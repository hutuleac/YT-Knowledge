Contested UNRESOLVED SINCE AN OCTOBER EVENT REFERRED TO AS '10/10'

Crypto's liquidity has stalled since '10/10' — and neither speaker has a clean explanation why

Macellari says the industry still doesn't have a consensus explanation for whatever happened around '10/10,' but the more durable story is a comedown from an unsustainable run of positive regulatory news plus AI absorbing the marginal risk-capital buyer that used to go to crypto.

  • Macellari: 'I don't feel like we really understand as a community what happened 10/10' — she's heard many after-the-fact explanations but none that fully resolve it for her.
  • Notable contrast with past cycles: despite fund drawdowns and withdrawals, this time there was no mass wave of forced liquidations/bankruptcies surfacing — she reads this as a sign of real market maturity versus 2018 or 2022.
  • From the Bitcoin ETF launch through last summer, crypto was on what she calls a 'sugar high' of continuous unprecedented regulatory news (new SEC/CFTC leadership, SAB 121 rollback) — a pace she argues was never sustainable, so the current lull is partly a comedown, not a collapse.
  • AI has pulled the marginal crypto buyer's risk appetite into AI equities instead — her analogy: the marginal buyer of the SpaceX IPO is likely also a Solana holder, and that capital has been going to AI, not crypto, over the past year.
  • Raoul's framing: excess liquidity (liquidity growth above GDP growth) has been weak, so what liquidity does exist has concentrated almost entirely into AI, and 'price begets price' — momentum has stayed with AI equities rather than rotating back to crypto.
  • Both agree the underlying crypto thesis (stablecoins, RWA tokenization, agentic payments) is strong even while current trading activity is weak — Raoul frames crypto's addressable market as having gone 'from 8 billion people to infinite agents.'

Active adoption blocker FRAGMENTING ACROSS PERMISSIONED, OPEN-SOURCE, AND MULTI-CHAIN RAILS

Stablecoins are recreating the 'too many streaming services' problem — and bad UX, not technology, is the real blocker

Competing coalitions building permissioned, open-source, and multi-chain stablecoin rails are fragmenting exactly the interoperability that made 'sending a dollar' the whole point — and both speakers agree crypto's historically terrible UX is the bigger adoption barrier than any of that.

  • Raoul flags the stablecoin market splitting into permissioned coalitions, open-source chains, and multi-chain deployments as actively undermining interoperability — market-share land-grabbing when, per Macellari, 'you'll all have market share, just let it grow.'
  • Macellari's cable-to-streaming analogy: stablecoins today feel like the early fragmented-streaming era — you remember a show exists, can't recall which of 12 services has it, cancel one, then have to resubscribe when it moves — and that experience won't drive mainstream adoption.
  • Self-deprecating admission of the UX problem: she describes spending until 3am on GitHub and Discord trying to recover $20 sent by mistake, and simply gave up — as a self-described 'scripted mom of three' who is unusually willing to try new tools, she says there's 'no path to adoption' for the average non-technical user under current UX.
  • Her prediction: the eventual stablecoin market leader will be whoever abstracts away the backend and bridging complexity entirely, the same way users don't think about which Wi-Fi network or OS a video call is running on.
  • Everybody who was going to get into crypto through code, or through pure hardcore determination, is already in — meaning further growth requires products built for people who won't tolerate friction, not more infrastructure for people who already will.

Contested REAL OPPORTUNITY, REAL MISUSE RISK

Tokenization-as-a-service got cheap fast — which makes 'who are you actually tokenizing this for?' the real question

What used to cost roughly $15M and 2.5 years to tokenize can now be done almost instantly and cheaply, but Macellari warns the technology can't manufacture real liquidity in illiquid assets — it can only relabel the risk, and retail buyers don't actually want what's being pitched to them.

  • Tokenization-as-a-service has advanced so far that a process once costing roughly $15M and around 100 people over 2.5 years is now close to instant and cheap — which shifts the hard question from technology/ops to pure business strategy: who are you trying to reach, and what do they actually want?
  • Observed mismatch: 'I've never met a real estate guy who has not said I'm interested in tokenization. But I've never met a retail investor who really cares about buying tokens on a share of some illiquid building' — she worries the industry is using tokenization to offload assets it has run out of natural buyers for.
  • Core structural point: 'it brings greater accessibility and the illusion of greater liquidity, but it doesn't actually create liquidity where there is none' — putting a non-marketable, gated fund on a blockchain doesn't change its underlying redemption terms, it just wraps them.
  • Real risk case: repackaging a fund's economic interest into a synthetic secondary market without changing the underlying terms — especially if combined with leveraged/looped structures — creates a 'powder keg' of counterparty risk investors don't understand they've taken on.
  • Fund managers themselves may push back: private equity/alternatives PMs deliberately want zero daily mark-to-market and don't want an investor letter effectively 'stamped' to anyone who held a token for 30 seconds — that opacity is part of how those strategies work, not an oversight to be fixed.
  • What should tokenize, per Raoul: liquid, homogeneous assets and yield/fixed-income products, since blockchain is the fastest and most efficient way to transfer and custody those — not illiquid real estate as a first move.

High conviction BOARD-LEVEL INBOUND SHARPLY UP OVER THE LAST 6 MONTHS

Institutional tokenization interest is accelerating fast — except among the commodity trading firms who'd benefit most

Macellari has gone from zero tokenization board requests to 15-20 presentations in six months, and Robin Hood is forcing both traditional finance and crypto-native platforms to move faster — but the multinational commodity trading firms with the clearest efficiency case have oddly not adopted stablecoin rails at all.

  • Macellari has done 15-20 board presentations on tokenization and stablecoins for large institutional clients in the past 6 months, versus having never been asked before that — inbound requests are arriving independently of each other across different boards.
  • Robin Hood is applying competitive pressure on both sides of the industry simultaneously — pushing traditional platforms and pushing crypto-native platforms like Coinbase (described as 'pretty slow for what should be a more nimble organization') to move faster.
  • Demographic signal: consistent study-after-study data shows younger users skew toward holding more digital assets than traditional mutual funds, and Robin Hood's user base is now maturing into marriage/retirement life stages, pushing the platform into territory historically owned by traditional wealth managers.
  • Even after a fund is tokenized, institutional buyers (not retail) still need to build out their own crypto rails before they can actually purchase it — Macellari cites ongoing work with Franklin Templeton's Sandy Kaul as an example of how slow but real this education process is.
  • Surprising laggard: multinational commodity trading firms with obvious efficiency cases for internal stablecoin cash management (getting capital unstuck across dozens of currencies and jurisdictions) reportedly aren't looking at it at all, per a friend who heads trading at a major commodities firm — despite Louis Dreyfus and other soft-commodity houses already using smart contracts for letters of credit since 2016-17.
  • Proposed explanation for the gap: sell-side banks make more money pitching multinational treasury desks currency options and FX products than they would pitching stablecoin efficiency that cannibalizes that revenue — 'there's always somebody being paid to recycle the capital in a different way.'
  • Brazil flagged as an unusually crypto-forward market that doesn't get enough credit for how far ahead it is on this front.

High conviction concern FLAGGED BY BOTH SPEAKERS, DIVERGING TEMPERAMENT ON HOW WORRIED TO BE

A failed 2021 push for shared digital-ID standards, AI labs self-reporting their own 'breakouts,' and a wargame that always goes nuclear

Macellari describes trying to get Amazon, Meta, LinkedIn, Microsoft, and Google to agree on a shared on-chain digital identity standard back in 2021 — everyone agreed on the coming problem and predicted their own decade of lawsuits, then refused to agree on interoperable credentials anyway.

  • In 2021 Macellari convened senior people from Amazon, Meta, LinkedIn, Microsoft, and Google, warned them AI-driven fake content impersonating real people was coming and they'd 'get sued like the banks did after 2008 — and you'll be sued for a decade over this,' and every one of them agreed with the prediction.
  • Despite that agreement, none would commit to a shared credentialing standard — she quotes the response as Microsoft refusing to accept Amazon's credentials — leaving digital identity/provenance as a still-unsolved gap she says should be 'top of mind' given how central it now is to distinguishing real from AI-generated content.
  • She's uneasy about AI labs' voluntary self-reporting of safety incidents ('something's escaped the lab') as effectively the only disclosure mechanism in place, given there's currently no way to meaningfully regulate frontier AI development at a government level.
  • Cites an Imperial War College study run during the Iran conflict period, testing multiple AI models in war-game scenarios: roughly 9 out of 10 times, models escalated to nuclear weapons use within 3-4 steps as the 'optimal' outcome — and inputting explicit civilian-casualty costs didn't change that outcome.
  • Referenced comparison point: intelligence capability is compounding on something closer to Reed's Law than Metcalfe's Law, with efficiency of intelligence output described as doubling roughly every 5 months — framed as an unprecedented pace no government can regulate in time.
  • Temperament divide between the two speakers: Macellari (fixed-income/distressed-debt background) says her instinct trends toward worst-case ('the machines are going to kill us all' is, she jokes, where fixed-income people's minds go), while Raoul says he's more optimistic that 'normally the good outcome prevails' and has chosen to lean into the trend rather than resist it.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Basis trade — A trade capturing the price spread between a crypto asset's spot price and its futures price.
Cross-exchange arbitrage — Profiting from the same asset trading at different prices simultaneously across different exchanges.
Market-neutral fund — A fund structured to have no net directional exposure to an asset's price, profiting instead from relative-value or arbitrage spreads.
RWA (real-world assets) — Traditional off-chain assets (bonds, real estate, credit) represented as on-chain tokens.
Tokenization-as-a-service — Pre-built infrastructure that lets a firm issue a tokenized fund or asset quickly without building custom blockchain tooling from scratch.
DTCC — The Depository Trust & Clearing Corporation, which clears the vast majority of US securities transactions off-chain today.
Reed's Law — A network-value law suggesting value scales exponentially (2^n) with group-forming potential, faster than Metcalfe's Law's n² — invoked here to describe AI capability compounding.
