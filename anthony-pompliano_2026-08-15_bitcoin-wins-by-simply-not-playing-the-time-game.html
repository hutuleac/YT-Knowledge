The AI trade bounced hard — and the lesson isn't the bottom, it's the Bayesian reset

Everyone hates AI, which is exactly why it's going higher: earnings are compounding above 30% while the index compresses, and the rally is running on the low volume that follows real capitulation.

  • Earnings are heading for 30%+ year-over-year while the S&P annualizes around 20% — multiple compression, with rate hikes priced in and two-year rates still well above Fed funds.
  • The macro prints are soft — negative payrolls, weak PPI, soft retail sales — and Visser's read is that the economy is fine but 'most of the good is happening inside AI.'
  • Situational awareness was a genuine market-clearing event: hedge funds down 25–30% for the month, heavy volume on the way out, and now a rally on low volume — the classic capitulation signature.
  • He explicitly rejects the framing that he called a bottom: 'there's no such thing as calling bottoms or calling tops.' What changes is risk/reward, not timing.
  • Micron is the worked example — he sold after an eight-bagger, never saw it trade back below his exit, and re-bought in the $700s anyway because the information changed: better earnings, long-term agreements that break the cyclical pattern, rising DRAM prices, and compute demand that accelerated despite open-source fears.
  • His 22V thematic AI portfolio runs ~100 names with a 10-name most-correlated subset that has retraced more than half the fall; disclosed components include Nvidia, Intel (CPUs rather than GPUs), Marvell on the optical side, plus Caterpillar and Corning.
  • The uncomfortable part for latecomers: there have been no corrections since, so nobody is being handed a re-entry point. His own stated horizon is to ride this five years, on the grounds that demand is still greater than it was at the start of the year.

Inflation is far weaker than the context says it should be — and AI may be why

Context is the whole argument: with tariffs, an ongoing war, fertilizer and nominal GDP acceleration all pushing the same way, core CPI should not be sitting at 2.5%.

  • Core CPI went from 2.6% at the end of last year to roughly 2.5% now — essentially the lows — and he cross-checks it against Truflation, trimmed mean and his own weekly composite rather than one series.
  • His framing isn't that inflation is above target, it's that in context it should be dramatically higher — and isn't. That gap is the signal.
  • He attributes part of it to AI deflation arriving earlier than expected: startup insurers like Lemonade undercutting on price as AI-improved margins let competitors pass savings through.
  • Oil sitting near $65 with the war unresolved is the other anomaly. His explanation is forward-looking: pipelines are being built, so a Hormuz disruption prices into one-year oil but not three-year oil.
  • On deportations as an inflation input — ~350,000 removals so far in FY2026, ~65,000 still detained, roughly 1,400–1,450 arrests a day in June, extrapolating past 400,000 by September 30 — he declines to call the direction: too many variables moving simultaneously to isolate one.
  • He's openly skeptical of the statistics themselves: 'those are published by someone for some reason,' and a competing number exists for the opposite purpose.
  • Where he does commit: on a three-year horizon inflation faces headwinds pushing it higher — he anchors on where things land in three years, not this month's print.

The middlemen are the S&P 500 — and tokenized finance is eating their float

A cash house purchase still takes 30 to 60 days for no reason a computer would recognize; every day of that friction is GDP and earnings that never happen.

  • Visser cites Figure's latest results at over 100% revenue growth and more than 50% EBITDA growth year-over-year — a 'rule of 150' he says only about two other large public companies meet. He offers Eli Lilly growing revenue ~50% at trillion-dollar scale as the scale comparison.
  • The origin story matters: Figure pitched a decentralized DTCC, built it, and found nobody would use it — so Mike Cagney originated the loans himself to prove one side of the marketplace.
  • What actually made it work wasn't blockchain or even AI: Plaid bank-account connections that verify income and employer, enabling a HELOC decision in five minutes and money in about five days. That got them to third-largest HELOC originator in the country.
  • The pattern generalizes — Robinhood now runs seven or eight separate business lines each doing $100M+ in revenue, and Stripe is the same shape: technologists ripping out inefficiency and dropping the cost of delivery.
  • The mechanism he keeps returning to is velocity, crediting Caitlin Long: if the same transaction volume clears in a quarter instead of a year, GDP and earnings both re-rate — and gradual deflation comes with it.
  • He's productizing the view — an index converging crypto tokens by vertical with the already-public names: Figure, Robinhood, PayPal, Coinbase. He expects Clarity Act regulatory clarity to be what finally brings hedge funds in, on a two-to-three-year horizon. His framing of the whole thread: 'Mark my words, you will all be paying attention to crypto a year from now.'

Everything is priced off human time — Bitcoin is the one thing that isn't

ARR, year-over-year, quarterly earnings: every unit investors use is a human-time convention, and AI is now moving fast enough to break the convention rather than beat it.

  • The title claim: Bitcoin is 'the S&P 500 of 10 years from now' not for any innovation reason, but because it doesn't suffer from time when everything else is measured against it.
  • ARR is the tell — an expectation of ~$120B ARR for Anthropic by year end implies a ~$10B final month, an annualization of something changing far too fast to annualize.
  • The valuation risk is inverted from how it's usually framed: the danger isn't Anthropic failing to reach a trillion, it's landing at 'only' $200B next year, because the derating happens the moment the growth rate visibly slows.
  • Multiple compression traces back to terminal value: anything not growing near AI's pace gets marked down. He cites Palantir's revenue growing 149% as the bar that resets expectations for everyone else. The corollary he draws is that the faster the world moves, the sooner today's information is irrelevant — new news arrives before the old is processed.
  • Anthropic's revenue, on his read, is dominated by Fortune 500 middlemen trying to replace employees — while AI-native businesses don't use frontier models that way, and specialized open-source models trained on your own data probably win for them.
  • That doesn't make frontier labs optional: you need models reaching for 2,000 IQ for governments, science and genuinely complex systems, even though roughly 150 IQ is all you need to replace a human worker.
  • The link back to tokenization: agentic transaction speed needs wallets, money and guardrails to exist first — which is why the DeFi rails and the time thesis are one argument, not two. Stablecoin volumes are already running at paces never seen before.

The moms have the agents now — and that's the tailwind for the trade

Pompliano's non-technical adoption indicator: his wife went from using AI as a supercharged Google to running a household-manager agent that texts her the day's list every morning.

  • The agent handles to-dos, upcoming events and remembered context — reducing mental load and, in his words, connecting dots she found genuinely useful. 'When you get to the moms, it's over.'
  • He pairs it with the homeschooling mother generating coloring books and lesson plans — evidence the utility has crossed from enthusiasts into ordinary daily use.
  • The sentiment shift is the investable part: Facebook and Anthropic have softened their language toward 'team human,' and public opinion is moving from blanket opposition to nuance.
  • Pompliano's conclusion: less resistance is a massive tailwind for AI stocks, and the foundation for another two to three years of the trade.
  • Visser's usage is conversational rather than transactional — he talks to Grok in the car, and treats a model that never tires of 'but why?' as the point. Many of the charts in his research are AI-generated.
  • His parenting frame: asking a model to explain the pro and con of something to a six-year-old implicitly teaches children how to think, versus school's 'the answer is C, memorize it for Friday.'
  • The caution he attaches: don't treat it as a therapist — treat it as conversation. He links rising childhood anxiety to technology displacing conversation, and notes X, TikTok and Instagram deliver judgment rather than relaxation.

The market moves on earnings — everything on the street corner is noise

Visser's filter is blunt: if you're investing more because of ICE, or expecting the country to blow up because of it, you're both wrong — the market is moving on earnings and AI.

  • He treats political sentiment as an artifact, not a signal: University of Michigan consumer confidence sits at all-time lows mostly because partisan readings would simply flip under a different president.
  • Bernie Sanders' statement on AI is acknowledged as a real political risk to Anthropic and OpenAI, but his operating question stays mechanical — are the data centers still being plugged in? Yes. Will we grow? Yes.
  • The Texas slowdown drew a notably cooperative response from physical builders — Hut 8 and a second data center developer publicly agreeing to work with the state — versus software AI companies that have been abrasive toward government.
  • Pompliano's read on why: building physical things tethers you to reality. You can build or you can't, so collaboration beats confrontation — a contrast with New York's effective blanket ban.
  • Visser separates the analyst from the parent without pretending otherwise: he defends voters' right to object and says he'd personally move his kids away from a data center, applying the same framing he used to vaccines — insufficient time to know.
  • His conclusion is that friction is the feature: disagreement forces the next idea, which is what creative destruction actually is. Even orbital data centers already draw debris objections — 'humans are never going to be happy.'
  • Field observation from both: micro-protests in New York and Maine running from seven to thirty people, well-produced signage, and skewing middle-aged rather than young.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Multiple compression — Earnings rising faster than the share price, so the valuation multiple shrinks — the S&P's 30%+ earnings growth against ~20% index performance. Visser traces it back to terminal value: anything not growing near AI's pace gets marked down.
Bayesian mindset — Updating your view as data arrives rather than defending a prior call. Visser's stated standard: criticize people for being stubborn, not for being wrong.
Rule of 150 — Revenue growth plus EBITDA growth exceeding 150 — Visser's shorthand for Figure's 100%+ and 50%+ combination, which he says only about two other large public companies achieve.
Velocity of money — How fast a unit of currency changes hands. Credited here to Caitlin Long: if tokenized settlement clears in minutes what takes 30–60 days, transaction count rises and both GDP and earnings re-rate.
HELOC — Home equity line of credit — borrowing against a house you already own. Figure's five-minute approval built on Plaid bank connections took it to third-largest US originator.
ARR — Annual recurring revenue, usually a monthly figure multiplied by twelve. Visser's objection is that it's a human-time convention applied to companies changing far too fast to annualize meaningfully.
Capitulation signature — Heavy volume on the way down followed by a rally on light volume — evidence that forced sellers are gone and demand absorbed supply at lower prices, rather than that buyers are enthusiastic.
Clarity Act — The regulatory framework Visser expects to be the unlock that finally brings hedge funds into crypto — on a two-to-three-year timeline, not immediately.
